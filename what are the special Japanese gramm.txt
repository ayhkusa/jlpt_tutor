what are the special Japanese grammars a new learner should be aware

If you're building a Japanese beginner/JLPT tutor, I would teach **Japanese grammar as a set of “special rules” that differ significantly from English**, rather than just as a list of conjugations.

Here are the most important ones for a new learner.

### 1. Particles determine the role of a word

Japanese relies heavily on particles rather than word order.

> 私**は**学生**です。
> I am a student.

The major beginner particles:

| Particle | Main function              | Example      |
| -------- | -------------------------- | ------------ |
| は        | topic                      | 私**は**学生です   |
| が        | subject/emphasis           | 猫**が**います    |
| を        | direct object              | 本**を**読みます   |
| に        | destination/time/existence | 学校**に**行きます  |
| で        | location of action / means | 学校**で**勉強します |
| と        | with / quotation           | 家族**と**食べます  |
| の        | possession/relationship    | 私**の**本      |
| も        | also                       | 私**も**学生です   |
| から       | from/because               | 東京**から**来ました |
| まで       | until/to                   | 5時**まで**     |
| へ        | toward                     | 学校**へ**行きます  |

**This is probably the single most important concept for an English-speaking beginner.**

---

### 2. Japanese normally omits the subject

Japanese doesn't constantly say **I, you, he, she**, etc.

> 毎日、日本語を勉強します。
> I study Japanese every day.

Literally, there is no "I" in the sentence.

Context determines who is doing the action.

This is a major difference from English.

---

### 3. The verb usually comes at the end

English:

> I eat dinner.

Japanese:

> 私は晩ご飯を**食べます**。

Pattern:

**Subject → Object → Verb**

> 私は → 晩ご飯を → 食べます

This becomes particularly important with longer sentences.

---

### 4. Japanese has politeness levels

A beginner should learn the distinction between:

**食べる** — eat (plain)

**食べます** — eat (polite)

**食べました** — ate (polite past)

**食べません** — don't eat

**食べませんでした** — didn't eat

The **ます form** is the safest conversational starting point for beginners.

---

### 5. Verbs don't change according to the subject

English:

> I eat
> He eats
> They eat

Japanese:

> 私は食べます。
> 彼は食べます。
> みんな食べます。

The verb remains **食べます**.

This makes Japanese conjugation easier in some ways.

---

### 6. Japanese adjectives behave differently

There are two major types.

**い-adjectives**

> 大きい — big
> 大きくない — not big
> 大きかった — was big

**な-adjectives**

> きれい — clean/beautiful
> きれいじゃない — not clean
> きれいでした — was clean

A new learner often assumes every adjective behaves like English adjectives. It doesn't.

---

### 7. です is not simply "is"

Beginners often learn:

> です = is

But that's an oversimplification.

> 学生です。
> I am a student.

> 静かです。
> It is quiet.

But:

> 食べです ❌

You don't attach です directly to a normal verb.

Understanding **です vs ます** early prevents many mistakes.

---

### 8. あります vs います

Japanese distinguishes between **inanimate** and **animate** existence.

**あります** → things

> 本があります。
> There is a book.

**います** → people/animals

> 猫がいます。
> There is a cat.

This distinction is particularly important for beginners.

---

### 9. に has many different meanings

This is one of the hardest beginner concepts.

> 7時**に**起きます。
> I wake up **at** 7.

> 学校**に**行きます。
> I go **to** school.

> 机の上**に**本があります。
> There is a book **on** the desk.

So don't teach **に = to**.

It's better to teach **に as a relationship marker whose meaning depends on the construction.**

---

### 10. で has multiple meanings too

> 学校**で**勉強します。
> Study **at** school.

> 箸**で**食べます。
> Eat **with** chopsticks.

> 電車**で**行きます。
> Go **by** train.

The common idea is often **the setting or means of an action**.

---

### 11. は and が are not interchangeable

This is one of the most important—and eventually difficult—Japanese grammar concepts.

> 私**は**学生です。

> 私**が**学生です。

Both can translate roughly as:

> I am a student.

But the emphasis/context differs.

A beginner doesn't need the full linguistic explanation immediately. Initially, teach:

**は → topic**

**が → subject / identification / emphasis**

Then introduce the deeper distinction later.

---

### 12. The て-form is extremely important

The て-form isn't just one grammar pattern. It becomes a **building block for many constructions**.

> 食べ**て**います
> am eating

> 食べ**て**ください
> please eat

> 食べ**ても**いいです
> may eat

> 食べ**ては**いけません
> must not eat

> 本を読ん**で**、寝ます。
> I read a book and go to sleep.

For an N4 tutor, I would make the **て-form a major milestone**.

---

### 13. Japanese has counters

Instead of simply saying:

> three books

Japanese uses a counter:

> 本**を3冊**買いました。

Different things use different counters:

* 人 — people
* 本 — long cylindrical objects
* 枚 — flat objects
* 冊 — books
* 台 — machines/vehicles
* 匹 — small animals
* 個 — general small objects

And counters can cause pronunciation changes:

> 一人（ひとり）
> 二人（ふたり）
> 三人（さんにん）

This is an area where memorization is unavoidable.

---

### 14. Numbers can change pronunciation

Japanese counters create many sound changes:

> 一個 — いっこ
> 六個 — ろっこ
> 八個 — はっこ

> 一本 — いっぽん
> 三本 — さんぼん
> 六本 — ろっぽん

So learners shouldn't assume that Japanese numbers are always pronounced independently.

---

### 15. Relative clauses come BEFORE the noun

English:

> the book **that I bought**

Japanese:

> **私が買った**本

Literally:

> **I bought** + book

This becomes extremely important around N4/N3.

Another example:

> 昨日買った本
> the book I bought yesterday

This is a major structural difference from English.

---

### 16. Japanese uses "modifier stacking"

You can keep putting information before a noun:

> 昨日駅で買った日本語の本

Literally:

> yesterday + at the station + bought + Japanese + book

Meaning:

> the Japanese book I bought at the station yesterday

Japanese can therefore build very long noun phrases before you reach the main noun.

---

### 17. Questions don't require inversion

English:

> Do you eat sushi?

Japanese:

> 寿司を食べますか。

It's basically:

> Sushi eat?

Add **か** to a polite statement to make it a question.

---

### 18. Japanese has different "I" and "you" choices

"I":

* 私（わたし）
* 僕（ぼく）
* 俺（おれ）

"You":

* あなた
* 君（きみ）
* お前（おまえ）

They aren't simply interchangeable. **Politeness, gender, relationship, and situation matter.**

A beginner should learn **私** first and avoid overusing **あなた**.

---

### 19. Japanese has levels of politeness

Eventually learners encounter:

> 行く
> 行きます
> いらっしゃいます
> お越しになります

These can all relate to "go/come," but the social relationship changes the appropriate form.

For N5/N4, focus on:

**plain → polite → negative → past**

and introduce **keigo** much later.

---

### 20. Word order is more flexible than English

Because particles indicate grammatical roles:

> 私は本を読みます。

can sometimes be rearranged:

> 本を私は読みます。

The second sounds more emphatic/marked, but the particles still tell us who does what.

So Japanese is **particle-driven**, not purely word-order-driven.

---

## The most important ones for your JLPT Tutor

If I were designing your curriculum, I'd prioritize them like this:

### N5 foundation

**① Particles**
は / が / を / に / で / と / の / も

**② Basic sentence structure**
Subject → Object → Verb

**③ Omitted subjects**

**④ です / ます**

**⑤ Verb conjugation**

**⑥ い-adjectives / な-adjectives**

**⑦ あります / います**

**⑧ Basic に / で distinction**

**⑨ Questions with か**

**⑩ Basic て-form**

### N4 expansion

Then add:

**⑪ ている**

**⑫ てください**

**⑬ てもいい**

**⑭ てはいけない**

**⑮ たり～たり**

**⑯ ないで**

**⑰ と思います**

**⑱ ので / から**

**⑲ Relative clauses**

**⑳ Potential form**

**㉑ ～たことがある**

**㉒ ～ながら**

**㉓ ～すぎる**

**㉔ ～やすい / ～にくい**

**㉕ Transitive vs. intransitive verbs**

That last one—**transitive vs. intransitive verbs**—is particularly important because it introduces a distinctly Japanese way of thinking about actions and states:

> ドア**を**開けました。
> I opened the door.

vs.

> ドア**が**開きました。
> The door opened.

For the kind of **vocabulary → example sentence → grammar explanation** system you're building, I would make these grammar concepts explicit metadata attached to every generated sentence. That would allow your app to say not just **"this is an N4 sentence"**, but **"this sentence teaches テーブル + で (location of action) + を + ます."**
