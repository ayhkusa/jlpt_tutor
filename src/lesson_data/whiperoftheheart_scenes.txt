1. Scene: Shizuku comes home

One of the first useful expressions is:

ただいま。
Tadaima.
"I'm home."

This is extremely common Japanese.

When someone comes home:

ただいま。 — I'm home.

The person already at home normally responds:

おかえり。 — Welcome home.

Important distinction

These aren't simply "hello" and "goodbye."

Japanese	Meaning	When
ただいま	I'm home	Person arriving home
おかえり	Welcome home	Person already at home
いってきます	I'm leaving / See you	Person leaving home
いってらっしゃい	See you / Take care	Person staying home

These four expressions are worth memorizing as a set.

Shizuku has gone out to buy milk.

牛乳（ぎゅうにゅう）
= milk

Notice the pronunciation:

牛乳 → ぎゅうにゅう
gyūnyū

The う lengthens the preceding vowel:

ぎゅ = gyu
ぎゅう = gyū

So don't pronounce it gyu-nyu too quickly.

3. 「〜なのに」 — even though / despite

The family conversation gives you a very useful structure involving the fact that she bought a plastic bag for only one item.

A natural example based on the scene is:

牛乳一本なのに、袋をもらった。
ぎゅうにゅう いっぽんなのに、ふくろを もらった。
"Even though it was only one carton of milk, I got a bag."

Grammar

Noun + なのに

means:

even though / despite the fact that...

Examples:

日曜日なのに、学校があります。
Even though it's Sunday, there is school.

子供なのに、とても大人です。
Even though he's a child, he's very mature.

This is useful N4 grammar.

4. 「だって〜もの」 — Because...

Shizuku responds casually to her mother's comment.

A very characteristic conversational pattern is:

だって〜もの。

It means something like:

"But...!"
"Because..."
"It's just that..."

For example:

だって、くれるんだもの。
"Because they give it to me."

This is much more conversational and emotional than simply saying:

なぜなら〜

Important

もの here isn't the ordinary noun "thing."

Compare:

これは私のものです。
This is mine.

versus:

だって、くれるんだもの。
"But they give it to me!"

The second もの expresses a reason/excuse.

5. 「〜んだ」 — explaining something

This is one of the most important Japanese conversational patterns you'll encounter in the movie.

For example:

くれるんだもの。

contains:

くれる + のだ → くれるんだ

In conversation:

のだ → んだ

It gives the feeling of:

"It's because..."
"The thing is..."
"You see..."

Compare:

雨です。
It's raining.

with:

雨なんです。
It's that it's raining / You see, it's raining.

The second sounds more like an explanation.

N4 pattern

Verb + んです

食べるんです。
"I'm going to eat." / "The thing is, I'm eating."

勉強するんです。
"I'm studying." / "You see, I'm studying."

好きなんです。
"I like it." / "The thing is, I like it."

6. Family conversation

The opening is particularly useful because the Japanese is ordinary family Japanese, rather than textbook Japanese.

You'll hear short commands and casual expressions.

For example:

早く。
はやく。
"Hurry."

ちょうだい。
"Give me [it]."

ありがと。
"Thanks."

Notice that ありがと is the casual spoken version of:

ありがとう。

And family members frequently omit subjects.

Japanese might say:

早くして。

rather than:

あなたは早くしてください。

The second is grammatically possible but sounds far too formal for a family conversation.

7. 「〜たら」 — when / after / if

The movie also gives you everyday examples of instructions involving what happens after something is done.

The pattern:

Verb た-form + ら

can mean:

when / after / if

For example:

ご飯を食べたら、勉強します。
ごはんを たべたら、べんきょうします。
"After I eat, I'll study."

Or:

シャワーを浴びたら、寝ます。
シャワーを あびたら、ねます。
"After I take a shower, I'll go to bed."

This is an important N4 grammar point.

11. 「〜ちゃった」 — accidentally / unfortunately / completely

Another important conversational grammar point you'll encounter throughout the movie is:

〜ちゃった

This is the casual contraction of:

〜てしまった

For example:

忘れちゃった。
わすれちゃった。
"I forgot."

Literally:

忘れてしまった

The contraction:

忘れてしまった → 忘れちゃった

Why is it important?

It often conveys:

accidentally did something
unfortunately did something
ended up doing something
completely finished something

Examples:

食べちゃった。
"I ate it."

忘れちゃった。
"I forgot."

寝ちゃった。
"I fell asleep."

This is extremely common spoken Japanese.

Minutes ~90–100 | Relationships, descriptions, and natural conversation

Let's continue with the next approximate 10-minute segment. At this point, I'll emphasize something especially important for understanding Whisper of the Heart: Japanese often communicates relationships and feelings indirectly, rather than stating everything explicitly.

1. 「仲がいい」— get along well

仲（なか） = relationship / relationship between people

仲がいい
なかが いい
= get along well / be close

Examples:

二人は仲がいい。
ふたりは なかが いい。
"The two get along well."

友達と仲がいい。
ともだちと なかが いい。
"I'm close with my friend."

Opposite

仲が悪い（なかが わるい）

= don't get along / have a bad relationship

2. 「友達」— friend

友達（ともだち）

= friend

Plural can be expressed through context, or:

友達たち

= friends / group of friends

But normally, Japanese doesn't need to explicitly mark plural.

友達が来た。

could mean:

"A friend came."

or:

"My friends came."

Context tells you.

3. 「付き合う」— date / associate with

This is an important word because it has several meanings.

付き合う（つきあう）

can mean:

associate with someone
spend time with someone
date someone
go along with someone

Romantic context:

彼と付き合っている。

"She's dating him."

But:

友達に付き合う。

can mean:

"Go along with a friend."

So don't automatically translate 付き合う as "date."

4. 「〜ている」 and relationships

When describing an ongoing relationship:

付き合っている

literally:

付き合う → 付き合っている

= "are dating"

Similarly:

住んでいる

= live

働いている

= work

知っている

= know

好きになっている

= have come to like

This is why 〜ている is so important.

It doesn't always mean simply "currently doing."

5. 「知ってる」— I know

You've probably noticed:

知っています

becomes:

知ってる

in casual speech.

Full:

知っている

Casual:

知ってる

Negative:

知らない

"I don't know."

Notice something interesting:

知っている = know
知らない = don't know

Not:

❌ 知ってない

Although 知ってない can occur in some contexts, 知らない is the normal basic negative form.

6. 「知ってる？」— Do you know?

Very common:

知ってる？

"Do you know?"

Polite:

知っていますか？

You may also hear:

知ってた？

"Did you know?"

This is:

知っていた → 知ってた

A very common contraction.

7. 「知らなかった」— I didn't know

This is an extremely useful phrase:

知らなかった。

"I didn't know."

Literally:

知らない → 知らなかった

This is one of those expressions worth memorizing as a complete phrase.

8. 「聞いた話」— a story I heard

Now let's combine the relative-clause grammar from Lesson 8.

聞いた話

= a story I heard

Breakdown:

聞いた
heard

話
story

So:

[I heard] + [story]

Similarly:

友達から聞いた話

= a story I heard from a friend

9. 「〜から聞いた」— heard from

Aから聞く

= hear from A

Example:

友達から聞いた。

"I heard it from a friend."

母から聞いた。

"I heard it from my mother."

先生から聞きました。

"I heard it from the teacher."

10. 「〜について」— about

Another essential N4 expression:

〜について

= about / concerning

Examples:

日本について話す。

"Talk about Japan."

映画について話した。

"I talked about the movie."

将来について考える。

"Think about the future."

This is particularly useful for discussing someone's dreams or plans.

11. 「について話す」

A useful combination:

〜について話す

= talk about ~

Examples:

学校について話す。

"Talk about school."

将来について話す。

"Talk about the future."

夢について話す。

"Talk about dreams."

12. 「どういう人？」— What kind of person?

You've already learned:

どういう意味？

Now change the noun:

どういう人？

= What kind of person?

Examples:

どんな人？

"What kind of person?"

どういう人なの？

"What kind of person is he/she?"

どういう vs どんな

Both can mean:

what kind of

どんな人？ is often simpler and more conversational.

13. 「優しい」— kind / gentle

A useful personality adjective:

優しい（やさしい）

= kind / gentle

Examples:

優しい人。

"a kind person"

彼は優しい。

"He's kind."

優しくない。

"He's not kind."

Adverb:

優しく話す。

"Speak gently."

14. 「面白い」has two meanings

We've already used:

面白い

= interesting

But it can also mean:

funny

Example:

この映画は面白い。

"This movie is interesting."

あの人、面白い。

"That person is funny/interesting."

Context determines the meaning.

15. 「変」— strange

Another useful description:

変（へん）

= strange / weird

Examples:

変な人。

"a strange person"

変な話。

"a strange story"

ちょっと変。

"That's a little strange."

Notice:

変な + noun

because 変 is a な-adjective.

16. 「普通」— normal / ordinary

普通（ふつう）

= normal / ordinary

Examples:

普通の人。

"an ordinary person"

普通だと思う。

"I think it's normal."

普通じゃない。

"It's not normal."

This can be used positively, neutrally, or negatively depending on tone.

17. 「変わっている」— unusual

A particularly useful expression:

変わっている（かわっている）

= unusual / unconventional / different

It comes from:

変わる（かわる）
= change

But when describing a person:

変わっている人

means:

an unusual person

It doesn't necessarily mean "bad."

It can mean:

eccentric / unconventional / different from others

18. 「ちょっと変わってる」

Very natural casual Japanese:

ちょっと変わってる。

= "They're a little unusual."

Notice:

変わっている → 変わってる

This is another example of:

〜ている → 〜てる

19. 「〜らしい」— apparently / apparently typical of

Another useful expression:

らしい

has several meanings, but one important use is:

apparently / I've heard that...

Example:

彼は学生らしい。

"Apparently he's a student."

It can also mean:

characteristic of

子供らしい。

"Childlike."

For now, focus on recognizing らしい as an indication that the speaker isn't necessarily stating something from direct knowledge.

20. 「そう」— looks/seems

Another word that can be confusing:

そう

Example:

おいしそう。

"Looks delicious."

楽しそう。

"Looks fun."

嬉しそう。

"Looks happy."

The pattern is:

adjective stem + そう

Examples:

おいしい → おいしそう

楽しそう

嬉しそう

This is extremely useful for describing what someone appears to feel.

21. 「〜そうに」— seemingly

When そう modifies a verb:

楽しそうに笑う。

= laugh happily / laugh as though having fun

嬉しそうに話す。

= speak happily / speak looking pleased

So:

そう

→ seems/looks

そうな + noun

→ a noun that looks/seems...

そうに + verb

→ do something looking/seeming...

22. 「〜そうだ」 vs 「〜みたい」

These are easy to confuse.

そうだ

Based on what you can see/hear:

雨が降りそう。

"Looks like it's going to rain."

みたい

Based more on appearance/impression:

雨みたい。

"Looks like rain."

They're similar, but the nuance differs.

For now, simply learn to recognize both.

23. 「どうせ」— anyway / in any case

A more advanced conversational word:

どうせ

= anyway / in any case

It often has a slightly negative or resigned feeling.

Example:

どうせ無理。

"It's impossible anyway."

どうせ分からない。

"I won't understand anyway."

You don't need to actively use this yet, but recognizing it will help with movie dialogue.

24. 「別に」— not particularly

Another word worth reinforcing:

別に

When answering a question:

別に。

can mean:

"Nothing."

"Not particularly."

"No reason."

Depending on tone, it can sound dismissive.

27. A useful conversation pattern

Imagine:

A: あの人、どういう人？
"What kind of person is that?"

B: ちょっと変わってるけど、優しい人だよ。
"He's a little unusual, but he's a kind person."

Breakdown:

ちょっと = a little
変わってる = unusual
けど = but
優しい = kind
人 = person
だよ = conversational assertion

This is a very natural style of Japanese.

28. 「だよ」— adding emphasis

You've heard です.

Casual:

だ

Adding よ:

だよ

often gives the feeling:

"I'm telling you..."

"You know..."

Example:

本当だよ。

"It's true, I'm telling you."

大丈夫だよ。

"It's okay."

優しい人だよ。

"He's a nice person."

Don't translate よ literally. It primarily signals that the speaker is presenting information to the listener.

29. 「ね」 vs 「よ」

These two are extremely important.

ね

seeks agreement:

いい天気だね。

"Nice weather, isn't it?"

よ

provides/emphasizes information:

いい天気だよ。

"The weather is nice, you know."

Together

いい天気だよね。

"It's nice weather, isn't it?"

You'll hear よ, ね, and よね constantly.

耳をすませば — Lesson 11
Minutes ~100–110 | Comparing, realizing, and making decisions
1. 「やっぱり」— after all / as I thought

やっぱり is one of the most useful conversational words in Japanese.

It comes from やはり, but やっぱり is much more common in casual speech.

Examples:

やっぱり行く。
"I'm going after all."

やっぱりそうだ。
"That's what I thought."

やっぱり難しい。
"It's difficult after all."

It often means the speaker has reached a conclusion that confirms an earlier feeling.

2. 「やっぱりそうだ」— Just as I thought

Breakdown:

やっぱり = as I thought / after all
そう = so / that way
だ = is

やっぱりそうだ。

"Just as I thought."

A very common variation:

やっぱりそうだった。

"So that's what it was."
"I knew it."

3. 「そう思う」— think so

そう思う。

= "I think so."

This is very common because Japanese frequently avoids repeating the entire statement.

For example:

A: この本は面白いと思う。
"I think this book is interesting."

B: 私もそう思う。
"I think so too."

Here そう replaces the entire preceding idea.

4. 「私も」— me too

Very simple but extremely important:

私も。

= "Me too."

Examples:

私もそう思う。

"I think so too."

私も行きたい。

"I want to go too."

私も知らない。

"I don't know either."

The particle も means approximately "also/too".

5. 「〜より」— than

Now let's learn comparison.

AよりB

= B more than A

Example:

日本語より英語が得意です。

"I'm better at English than Japanese."

The basic structure:

A より B が + adjective

6. 「〜のほうが」— ... is more

For a clearer comparison:

AよりBのほうが〜

Example:

電車よりバスのほうが安い。

"The bus is cheaper than the train."

Breakdown:

電車より
than the train

バスのほうが
the bus is more

安い
cheap

This is a very useful N4 pattern.

7. 「どちらのほうが？」— Which is more?

When comparing two things:

どちらのほうが好き？

"Which do you like better?"

Example:

映画と本と、どちらのほうが好き？

"Which do you like better, movies or books?"

You can answer:

本のほうが好き。

"I like books better."

8. 「一番」— the most

一番（いちばん）

= most / number one

Example:

これが一番好き。

"I like this one the most."

一番大切なこと

= the most important thing

一番好きな本

= my favorite book

Notice the relative clause:

一番好きな + noun

9. 「大切」— important / precious

大切（たいせつ）

= important / precious / valuable

It's a な-adjective.

Examples:

大切な人

"an important person / someone precious to me"

大切な本

"a precious book"

これは大切です。

"This is important."

10. 「大事」— important

Another very common word:

大事（だいじ）

= important / valuable

It's similar to 大切.

大事なこと

= an important thing

大切なこと

= an important thing

For your current level, you can generally treat them as close synonyms.

11. 「大切にする」— treasure / take good care of

This is a particularly useful expression.

大切にする

= cherish / take good care of

Examples:

本を大切にする。

"Take good care of books."

友達を大切にする。

"Treasure your friends."

家族を大切にする。

"Treasure your family."

This is different from simply:

大切です

which means "is important."

12. 「気づく」— notice / realize

An important verb:

気づく（きづく）

= notice / realize

Examples:

気づいた。

"I realized."

気づかなかった。

"I didn't notice."

今、気づいた。

"I just realized."

This is extremely common in stories.

13. 「気がつく」— notice / realize

You may also hear:

気がつく

which has essentially the same meaning.

気がついた。

"I noticed."

全然気がつかなかった。

"I didn't notice at all."

For now:

気づく ≈ 気がつく

14. 「初めて気づいた」— I realized for the first time

Combine two patterns:

初めて = for the first time

気づいた = realized

初めて気づいた。

= "I realized it for the first time."

You can make it more specific:

大切さに気づいた。

"I realized its importance."

The particle に is commonly used with 気づく for what you notice.

15. 「〜と思った」— I thought...

You've learned:

〜と思う
"I think..."

Past:

〜と思った
"I thought..."

Examples:

難しいと思った。

"I thought it was difficult."

簡単だと思った。

"I thought it was easy."

できると思った。

"I thought I could do it."

16. 「でも、違った」— but I was wrong

違う（ちがう）

= be different / be wrong

Past:

違った。

"It was different."

It can also mean:

"That's wrong."

違うよ。

"No, that's not right."

そうじゃない。

"That's not it."

These are very common conversational corrections.

17. 「本当に？」— Really?

You've probably heard this many times.

本当に？

= Really?

Casual:

本当？

= Really?

More emphatic:

本当に？

"Really?"

本当なの？

"Is that really true?"

18. 「嘘」— lie

嘘（うそ）

= lie

嘘でしょ？

"You're kidding, right?"

Literally:

"It's a lie, right?"

Very common conversational Japanese.

嘘！

"No way!"

This doesn't necessarily mean someone literally lied.

It can express surprise.

19. 「信じる」— believe

信じる（しんじる）

= believe / trust

Examples:

信じてる。

"I believe [you]."

信じられない。

"I can't believe it."

This second expression is particularly useful.

信じる → 信じられる

= can believe

信じられない

= cannot believe

20. 「信じられない！」— I can't believe it!

This can express either:

disbelief

"I can't believe it!"

or

amazement

"Unbelievable!"

Context and intonation determine which.

This is a very common movie expression.

21. 「大丈夫」— okay / all right

You've seen this before, but it's worth reinforcing because it has many uses.

大丈夫？

"Are you okay?"

大丈夫。

"I'm okay."

大丈夫だよ。

"It's okay."

大丈夫じゃない。

"I'm not okay."

It can also mean:

"No, thank you."

For example, if someone offers something:

コーヒーはいかがですか？

"Would you like coffee?"

大丈夫です。

"No, thank you."

Context matters enormously.

22. 「心配」— worry

心配（しんぱい）

= worry / concern

心配する

= worry

Example:

心配している。

"I'm worried."

Casual:

心配してる。

心配しないで。

"Don't worry."

This is a very useful expression.

23. 「心配しないで」— Don't worry

Breakdown:

心配する
worry

Negative て-form:

心配しないで

= don't worry

The grammar:

Verb negative + で

can be used to tell someone not to do something.

Examples:

心配しないで。

Don't worry.

忘れないで。

Don't forget.

行かないで。

Don't go.

24. 「忘れないで」— Don't forget

This is another extremely useful phrase.

忘れる
forget

忘れない
don't forget

忘れないで
don't forget

Example:

約束を忘れないで。

"Don't forget the promise."

25. 「約束」— promise

約束（やくそく）

= promise / appointment / agreement

Examples:

約束する。

"make a promise"

約束した。

"I promised."

約束を守る。

"keep a promise."

約束を忘れないで。

"Don't forget the promise."

26. 「守る」— protect / keep

守る（まもる）

has several important meanings:

protect
obey
keep a promise/rule

Examples:

約束を守る。

"Keep a promise."

ルールを守る。

"Follow the rules."

家族を守る。

"Protect the family."

耳をすませば — Lesson 12
Minutes ~110–120 | Decisions, advice, emotions, and natural speech

Let's continue with the next approximate 10 minutes. I'll keep the focus on Japanese that is useful beyond the movie—especially advice, obligation, uncertainty, emotional reactions, and conversational contractions.

I won't reproduce the film's dialogue verbatim.

1. 「どうしよう」— What should I do?

This is one of the most useful expressions in Japanese conversation.

どうしよう？

= "What should I do?"

It comes from:

どうする = what will/do you do?

→ どうしよう = what shall I do?

You'll hear this constantly in Japanese dramas and everyday conversation.

Example

明日どうしよう？

"What should I do tomorrow?"

これ、どうしよう？

"What should I do with this?"

2. 「どうしようかな」— I wonder what I should do

Add かな:

どうしようかな。

= "I wonder what I should do."

The かな makes it sound like you're thinking to yourself.

Compare:

どうしよう？

"What should I do?"

どうしようかな。

"Hmm... I wonder what I should do."

3. 「〜たほうがいい」— You should...

You've seen this before, but it's worth mastering.

Verb た-form + ほうがいい

= should do...

Example:

勉強したほうがいい。

"You should study."

早く寝たほうがいい。

"You should go to bed early."

Negative advice

Use:

Verb ない-form + ほうがいい

Example:

行かないほうがいい。

"You shouldn't go."

無理しないほうがいい。

"You shouldn't push yourself."

4. 「無理」— impossible / unreasonable / too much

無理（むり）

is an extremely common Japanese word.

Depending on context:

impossible
unreasonable
too difficult
can't do it
too much

Examples:

無理。

"Impossible."

"I can't."

無理しないで。

"Don't overdo it."

それは無理だよ。

"That's impossible."

5. 「無理する」— push yourself too hard

無理する

literally:

do something unreasonable

Natural meanings:

overdo it
push yourself
force yourself

無理しないで。

"Don't push yourself."

This expression is useful when someone is working too hard.

6. 「大丈夫だと思う」— I think it'll be okay

Combine:

大丈夫 + と思う

大丈夫だと思う。

"I think it'll be okay."

Notice that だ appears before と思う because 大丈夫 is a な-adjective.

Compare:

面白いと思う。

"I think it's interesting."

But:

大丈夫だと思う。

"I think it's okay."

7. 「〜と思っていた」— I had thought...

You've learned:

〜と思う
I think

〜と思った
I thought

Now:

〜と思っていた

= "I had thought..." / "I used to think..."

This describes a thought that existed for some period of time.

Example:

簡単だと思っていた。

"I thought it was easy."

But later you discovered it wasn't.

This is a very useful distinction.

8. 「〜たら」— if / when

You've already encountered:

〜たらいい

Now focus on the basic conditional:

Verb た-form + ら

Examples:

時間があったら、行く。

"If I have time, I'll go."

暇だったら、来て。

"If you're free, come."

できたら教えて。

"If you can, let me know."

9. 「できたら」— if you can

This is particularly common.

できたら

= if you can / if possible

Example:

できたら手伝って。

"If you can, help me."

できたら来て。

"If you can, come."

This is softer than simply giving a command.

10. 「もし〜たら」— if...

Adding もし emphasizes the condition:

もし時間があったら、行きます。

"If I have time, I'll go."

もし分からなかったら、聞いてください。

"If you don't understand, please ask."

The もし isn't always necessary.

時間があったら

already means:

"If I have time..."

11. 「〜なら」— if that's the case

Another conditional:

〜なら

= if that's the case / if it's...

Example:

日本へ行くなら、東京に行ってみて。

"If you're going to Japan, try visiting Tokyo."

暇なら、手伝って。

"If you're free, help me."

The nuance is slightly different from 〜たら.

For now:

〜たら → if/when something happens

〜なら → if that's the situation / if you're talking about...

12. 「それなら」— in that case

Very useful conversational expression:

それなら

= in that case / if so

Example:

明日は休みです。

"Tomorrow is a holiday."

それなら、映画を見よう。

"In that case, let's watch a movie."

You'll hear this frequently.

13. 「だったら」— then / if that's the case

Casual form:

だったら

= then / if that's the case

Example:

行きたい。

"I want to go."

だったら、行こう。

"Then let's go."

It can be more emphatic than それなら.

14. 「〜よう」— Let's...

You've already encountered the volitional form.

行こう。

"Let's go."

食べよう。

"Let's eat."

見よう。

"Let's watch."

やろう。

"Let's do it."

This is also used for the speaker's own intention:

頑張ろう。

"I'll do my best." / "Let's do our best."

15. 「一緒に」— together

一緒に（いっしょに）

= together

Examples:

一緒に行こう。

"Let's go together."

一緒に食べよう。

"Let's eat together."

一緒に勉強しよう。

"Let's study together."

Very common with the volitional form.

16. 「自分」— oneself / myself

自分（じぶん）

is interesting because it can refer to:

oneself
myself
yourself
himself/herself

depending on context.

Example:

自分でやる。

"I'll do it myself."

自分のこと

= one's own affairs / oneself

自分で決める。

"I'll decide for myself."

17. 「自分で」— by oneself

自分で

= by oneself / on one's own

Example:

自分で考えて。

"Think for yourself."

自分で決めた。

"I decided it myself."

The particle で here indicates the means/manner:

"by myself"

18. 「考え直す」— reconsider

A useful compound:

考える
think

考え直す
reconsider / think again

Examples:

もう一度考え直す。

"I'll reconsider."

考え直して。

"Think it over again."

The suffix:

〜直す

means:

do again / redo

19. 「やり直す」— do over

Same pattern:

やる → やり直す

= redo

Example:

もう一度やり直す。

"I'll do it again."

最初からやり直す。

"I'll start over from the beginning."

This is a very useful compound-verb pattern.

20. 「もう一度」— one more time

もう一度（もういちど）

= one more time / once again

Examples:

もう一度言ってください。

"Please say it again."

もう一度考える。

"I'll think about it again."

もう一度やってみる。

"I'll try it one more time."

21. 「本気」— serious / earnest

本気（ほんき）

= seriousness / being serious

本気で

= seriously

Example:

本気でやる。

"I'll do it seriously."

本気なの？

"Are you serious?"

本気で言ってる？

"Are you serious?"

This is very common conversational Japanese.

22. 「本気で」— seriously

Don't confuse:

本当に

= really

with:

本気で

= seriously / genuinely

Compare:

本当に好き。

"I really like it."

本気で好き。

"I seriously/genuinely like it."

The second carries more emotional commitment.

23. 「ちゃんと」— properly

Another extremely useful conversational word:

ちゃんと

= properly / correctly / as you should

Examples:

ちゃんと勉強して。

"Study properly."

ちゃんと食べて。

"Eat properly."

ちゃんと聞いて。

"Listen carefully."

ちゃんと分かってる。

"I understand properly."

You'll hear this constantly.

24. 「ちゃんとする」— do things properly

ちゃんとする

= behave properly / get yourself together / do things properly

Example:

ちゃんとして！

"Get it together!"

"Behave yourself!"

Depending on tone, this can be gentle or quite strong.

25. 「まだまだ」— still a long way to go

Let's reinforce this because it's particularly useful with the movie's theme of developing one's ability.

まだまだ。

= "Not yet." / "I've got a long way to go."

まだまだ頑張らないと。

"I still have to work hard."

Here:

〜ないと

is a casual contraction we'll look at next.

26. 「〜ないと」— have to

You've already learned:

〜なきゃ

= have to

Another common form:

〜ないと

It comes from:

〜ないといけない

= must / have to

Example:

勉強しないと。

"I have to study."

Full:

勉強しないといけない。

Casual shortened:

勉強しないと。

This is extremely common.

27. Three ways to say "I have to study"
Formal

勉強しなければならない。

Normal conversational

勉強しないといけない。

Very casual

勉強しなきゃ。

All mean approximately:

"I have to study."

This is an important listening skill because Japanese speakers frequently shorten the longer versions.

28. 「〜なくちゃ」— another "have to"

Yet another form:

勉強しなくちゃ。

= "I have to study."

So now recognize:

勉強しなければならない
↓
勉強しないといけない
↓
勉強しなきゃ
↓
勉強しなくちゃ

Different levels of casualness, but essentially the same basic idea.
